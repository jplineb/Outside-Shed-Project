spending money. It is not a code-compliance inspection, and photographs cannot establish concealed framing,

## Bottom line

The project is probably worth saving, but the next dollars should buy information and stop known water, not cover
uncertain details.

1. Remove fuel and overhead storage, clear the wall base, and wet-clean suspected animal waste.
2. Extend the known bad downspout discharge and keep soil/grass away from the siding.
3. During and after rain, map where water lands and enters. Record wood moisture at the same marked locations.
4. Expose and probe the sill, stud ends, sheathing edges, and anchor locations. Repair structure before sealing.
5. Make a sill/flashing detail from the exposed geometry. Do not blindly install either a skirt or an open screen.
6. Trace the blue line and inspect the roof/vent path before hiding the deck with foil.
7. Start room ventilation around 500-800 delivered CFM with both jalousies or a door open; measure before upsizing.
8. Install the radiant barrier only after roof leaks, wiring, and repairs are resolved.

## 1. Diagnose the wall base before designing the cover

The photographs show staining and debris at one interior wall section. They do not show the condition around the
full 56 ft perimeter, nor do they show the hidden relationship among siding, sheathing, sill, block, and slab.
That relationship controls the repair.

See [`diagrams/independent-sill-decision.png`](diagrams/independent-sill-decision.png).

### First observations

- Extend the downspout 4-6 ft to a point that drains away. This is cheap and the concentrated corner discharge is
  visible in the exterior photo.
- Remove objects against the siding and trim vegetation away so the bottom edge can be inspected and dry.
- Do not assume the unguttered eave is defective. Free-draining eaves are common. Watch it in rain: note roof runoff
  landing distance, ponding, splash height, grade direction, and whether the wall gets wet.
- Do not excavate a gravel strip until grade and foundation depth are known. A useful strip has coarse stone over
  geotextile, slopes away, and does not create a trench that collects water beside the block.
- Photograph and mark every damp area. A pin moisture meter provides a repeatable trend; compare suspect wood with
  known-dry wood of the same type. Meter readings are screening data, not a species-corrected laboratory result.

### Then expose and classify

Clean debris away and determine which condition actually exists:

**A. Siding overhangs a draining cavity.** Preserve drainage. Use corrosion-resistant insect screen at the opening
without packing the cavity solid. Any drip edge must be lapped behind the water-shedding layer, not face-caulked into
a trough.

**B. A horizontal gap exposes the sill or wall interior.** A formed metal flashing may be needed. Its upper leg must
go behind sound siding/sheathing or an intentional reglet; its lower hem must drip clear of the CMU face. Hardware
cloth is the pest component, not the water-management component.

**C. Sheathing/siding or sill is decayed.** Remove material until sound wood is reached. Support the wall as required,
repair plate/stud/sheathing sections, restore anchor bearing, add a capillary break where new wood contacts masonry,
then flash. Covering decayed wood with PVC, PT trim, foam, or metal does not repair it.

Pressure-treated wood is rot-resistant, not a substitute for drainage. Face-fastened skirts can trap debris and water;
an uncovered screen can admit wind-driven rain. Choose neither detail before seeing the section.

### How to assess wood

Wet wood does not automatically “lie” under a screwdriver. Use several indicators together:

- probe resistance compared with nearby known-sound wood;
- fibers that crush, flake, or can be excavated beyond the surface;
- remaining member dimensions and connection condition;
- moisture trend after the known water source is corrected;
- anchor washers that have lost firm bearing.

If decay is widespread, an anchor is loose, the wall has moved, or temporary support is needed, obtain an on-site
carpenter or engineer assessment before cutting a sill or stud.

## 2. Verify two separate air systems

See [`diagrams/independent-airflow-plan.png`](diagrams/independent-airflow-plan.png).

### Room ventilation - verified openings

The two front jalousies and double door are known openings. Put an exhaust fan high on the opposite wall or a gable,
with a rain hood, insect screen that can be cleaned, and a backdraft damper. Do not cut a stud or roof member to fit
the fan.

Estimated room volume is roughly 1,400-1,700 cu ft because roof rise is not measured:

| Target | Required delivered airflow |
|---|---:|
| 10 ACH | 235-285 CFM |
| 20 ACH | 470-570 CFM |
| 30 ACH | 700-850 CFM |

Fan labels are free-air ratings; screens, louvers, ducts, and static pressure reduce delivered flow. A controllable
500-800 CFM fan is a defensible first installation for this small workspace. Open both jalousies and, if the fan
struggles or doors pull hard, crack a door. Larger is not automatically better: noise, power, rain protection, and
makeup-air area matter.

Before and after changes, log outdoor shade temperature, indoor air temperature at chest height, roof-deck surface
temperature, humidity, time, and sun. Without the outdoor reading, neither 105 F nor a later lower number measures
the improvement fairly.

### Roof ventilation - not verified

Confirm all of these on site:

1. Is each rear louver in a horizontal eave soffit, a rake overhang, or the wall?
2. Can air pass from it into rafter bays, or is blocking in the way?
3. Is there a continuous ridge vent, isolated roof vent, or only daylight at an unintended gap?
4. Are there baffles or clear paths in every bay that will be covered?
5. Does any opening show water staining or insect entry?

Do not add “three or four vents” from gross vent dimensions. Products have a listed net free ventilating area, and
balanced intake/exhaust sizing requires the actual roof assembly and local requirements. A hole cut in the wrong
plane does not ventilate a rafter bay.

## 3. Radiant barrier: keep it conditional

The low-emittance surface needs an adjacent air space; it should not be bonded to the roof deck. The current
under-rafter concept is reasonable after the roof system is verified.

Before installation:

- trace both ends of the blue line and determine its function; use the correct tester for that system;
- inspect and repair roof leaks, deck damage, fasteners, and framing connections;
- remove stored material from horizontal roof members and do not plan to reuse them as storage;
- measure slope length and framing spacing, then include laps, cuts, and waste in quantity;
- preserve every verified ventilation and drainage path;
- maintain access to anything that will need service.

I would not recommend a flat foil ceiling from the photos. The horizontal members have not been identified or checked
as a continuous ceiling-support system, and a new attic boundary changes access, ventilation, and moisture behavior.

## 4. Cleanup and fire-safety corrections

- Fuel cans should live in a suitable detached fuel-storage location, not return to a hot bike/work shed merely
  because a fan was added.
- Ventilate the shed before cleanup, but do not aim a fan across dry droppings. Wet suspected droppings with a
  label-approved disinfectant, allow its stated contact time, remove them, and only then clean remaining dust.
- Use a HEPA-filtered vacuum for residual fine contamination. “A decent shop-vac filter” can discharge fine material.
- Do not routinely spread diatomaceous earth or boric acid into an open, damp gap. Dust is an inhalation exposure,
  dampness reduces effectiveness, and physical exclusion is the durable control.
- Never mix bleach with ammonia, acids, or other cleaners. Follow the product label rather than relying on one
  universal dilution.

## 5. Door and power

An astragal, threshold, and sweeps may improve the double doors, but first square the opening, identify hinge/latch
geometry, and repair soundness at the bottoms. A diagonal brace’s effective orientation depends on whether it acts in
compression or tension and how it is connected; a photo alone does not justify flipping it. Avoid creating a raised
threshold that becomes a bicycle trip hazard or sends rain inward.

For solar, make a load table first: watts, hours per day, simultaneous use, startup current, and whether each load can
run directly on DC. Then measure solar exposure and inspect remaining roof life. Keep batteries out of extreme heat
and use a charge controller with temperature limits appropriate to the battery chemistry. A portable, garage-charged
power source may remain cheaper than a permanent system for intermittent use.

## Revised decision sequence

1. **Make safe:** fuel out permanently; overhead stock down; PPE and wet cleanup.
2. **Stop known water:** downspout extension; vegetation and stored panels away from walls.
3. **Observe:** rain map, grade, full perimeter, moisture map, cable trace, roof/vent path.
4. **Repair structure:** sill/stud/sheathing/anchors/door as evidence requires.
5. **Detail the envelope:** draw the measured cross-section; then choose screen, flashing, drainage, and coatings.
6. **Ventilate the room:** 500-800 delivered-CFM starting range, controllable, weather-protected, with makeup air.
7. **Add radiant barrier:** only after hidden work and roof repairs are complete.
8. **Measure comfort:** compare simultaneous indoor/outdoor and surface readings; adjust fan or shading if needed.
9. **Add solar only if justified:** size from measured loads and safe battery temperature.

The key difference is not a more elaborate build. It is delaying irreversible covers and holes until the shed itself
answers the few questions the photos cannot.
