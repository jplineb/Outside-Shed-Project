## The short version

1. **The prime water suspect is wrong.** The notes blame the block-line gap and the front downspout. The bigger
   source is that the roof only has a gutter on one eave. Roughly 100 sq ft of roof sheds straight onto the ground
   in a line about a foot off the un-guttered wall — about 63 gallons per inch of rain, every storm.
2. **Drying is the only task with weeks of latency, so it has to start first.** Everything else in the envelope
   phase waits on wet wood. Move the water fixes to day one, before the junk-out.
3. **Probing wet wood lies to you.** Soaked softwood reads punky under a screwdriver when it is still sound. Probe
   after several dry weeks or you will buy lumber you did not need.
4. **The blue line on the roof deck is held by metal cable staples.** Zoomed in, there is a nail-through strap over
   a round blue jacketed line, repeated along the run. Chalk lines do not come with hardware. Trace it before you
   staple foil across those rafters.
5. **The back vents are in the sloped rake soffit of the gable overhang, not in an eave soffit.** They open a couple
   of feet from the ridge, so their air short-circuits straight back out the ridge vent. Neither 16 ft eave has any
   intake at all.
6. **Fan size is the heat lever, not the foil.** A 100 CFM vent fan in 1,400 cu ft is 4 air changes an hour and you
   will not feel it. You want 800 CFM minimum, ideally 1,500–2,000.
7. **The comfort target as written is not reachable.** Without AC or evaporation you cannot beat outdoor shade
   temperature. High 80s at midday in an SC summer is not on the table; ambient plus 3–5 °F is. The cheapest degrees
   in this whole project come from the clock, not from hardware.
8. **The lighter paint will not buy meaningful degrees.** Do it, but do it to protect the T1-11 and seal its raw
   bottom edge, and expect roughly 1–3 °F, not a fix.
9. **Do the foil early, not in phase 3.** It is a few hours and one roll once the collar ties are clear, and it makes
   every later work session survivable.
10. **The doors need an astragal and a threshold, not just a sweep.** There is a visible daylight gap between the
    leaves and no threshold at the slab.
11. **Solar: skip the inverter and skip the roof.** A panel wired to a DC fan and DC lights is load-matched to the
    exact hour you need it, and a ground or gable mount means you never chase a leak in a shingle roof.

## What I would not change

So you do not have to re-litigate these: perforated foil under the rafters with the bay left as an air gap; no bug
bomb; no swamp cooler in SC humidity; hardware cloth rather than foam as the pest seal; repair the door in place
rather than rebuilding the opening; battery tools only in the shed; nest before any eave work; probe before you seal;
junk out before you clean; keep solar small. All of that is right, and the reasoning in `NOTES.md` for each is sound.

## 1. Water: right fix, wrong prime suspect, wrong order

See [`diagrams/alt-water-first.png`](diagrams/alt-water-first.png).

The interior tide mark in [`photos/interior-sill.jpg`](photos/interior-sill.jpg) is a uniform 6–10 in band along the
wall base, not a puddle pattern radiating from one corner. A downspout dumping at one corner makes a local problem.
A uniform band along a whole wall is splash.

Here is what the exterior photos show that changes the diagnosis:

- **Only one eave has a gutter.** [`photos/exterior.jpg`](photos/exterior.jpg) shows the gutter and downspout on the
  door side. [`photos/exterior-back.jpg`](photos/exterior-back.jpg) shows the opposite side with a bare fascia and no
  gutter. Half the roof — about 100 sq ft — free-sheds in a line along that wall. One inch of rain over 100 sq ft is
  about 63 gallons.
- **The landing zone is 8–10 in below the siding.** The bottom of the T1-11 sits at roughly the top of the block, and
  grass is right up against the block face. Water hits grass and soil a foot from the wall and splashes back onto the
  bottom 6–10 in of siding — exactly the height of the interior stain.
- **The siding's bottom edge is raw.** T1-11 has a wood-composite core, and a cut bottom edge in a splash zone wicks
  like a straw. That is the most likely route from the outside splash to the inside stain.
- **There is a debris berm on the inside.** In the sill photo, soil and crumbly material are piled in the corner where
  the block ledge meets the plate. That berm holds water against the plate for days after a storm. Shovelling it out
  is free and worth more than any can of foam.
- **A large flat panel is leaning against the front wall.** In [`photos/exterior.jpg`](photos/exterior.jpg), the smooth
  grey panel to the left of the door is leaned against the siding, standing on the concrete apron. It traps water
  against the wall and it is almost certainly what is hiding the left jalousie in that photo. Pull it off the wall.

**What I would do differently:** fix water at the ground first, and do not add a second gutter. Your existing gutter is
where the yellow jackets nested and where the guards lifted. A 12–18 in gravel drip strip along the un-guttered eave and
gables cannot clog, nothing nests in it, needs no ladder, and costs about $30–45 in stone and fabric. Then move the
downspout, cut the grass line back off the siding, and prime the raw bottom edge of the siding.

**On the sill detail itself:** I would not install the Z-flashing or PT skirt. The inside of this wall is open framing,
so the plate can dry inward as long as it is clear and the shed gets ventilated. A skirt does not add drying — it adds a
debris trap and a void for wasps and mice. Screen the gap for pests, then leave it open and draining. And do not buy the
hardware cloth roll yet: the fix depends entirely on the gap width, which is still unmeasured. If it is a hairline you
cannot stuff anything into it and the money belongs in the gravel instead.

## 2. The blue line on the roof deck is a stapled cable

The locked decision says the blue mark in [`photos/interior-roof.jpg`](photos/interior-roof.jpg) is a layout or chalk
line. Zoomed to full resolution, there is a small galvanized nail-through strap sitting over a round blue jacketed line,
with a shadow under the line, and the straps repeat at intervals along the run — visible again in
[`photos/interior-rafters.jpg`](photos/interior-rafters.jpg) where two parallel courses cross the whole width. Chalk
lines do not get fastened down.

Blue jacket is not standard house wiring colour, so my guess is low voltage: an old landscape-lighting, phone, doorbell,
or fence lead. It may well be dead. But three reasons to settle it before phase 3:

- You are about to drive a few hundred staples into those rafters and stretch conductive foil across the whole run.
- If anything in it is live, "no power on site" is wrong, and that changes the solar plan entirely — you may already
  have a path to the building.
- A non-contact voltage tester is about $15 and this takes five minutes.

The yellow cord hanging from a joist in [`photos/interior.jpg`](photos/interior.jpg) is a separate thing — that one does
look like a leftover extension cord.

## 3. Those back vents are in the wrong plane to do what the notes assume

See [`diagrams/alt-two-air-loops.png`](diagrams/alt-two-air-loops.png).

`NOTES.md` treats the two louvers as eave soffit vents feeding the rafter bays: outside air in at the soffit, up each
bay, out the ridge. In [`photos/exterior-back.jpg`](photos/exterior-back.jpg) the louver is set into a **sloping** soffit
panel whose outer edge is the rake fascia with sky beyond it. That is the underside of the gable overhang, not a
horizontal eave soffit.

That matters because a vent in the rake opens near the top of the roof. Its air travels a couple of feet along the ridge
and leaves through the ridge vent. It never goes down a rafter bay. Combined with the front eave having no vents at all,
**neither 16 ft eave has intake**, so the vented air gap behind your foil has no supply anywhere along its length.

Two consequences:

- The advice "do not block the soffit path with foil" is less relevant than it looks, because there is no eave soffit
  path to block. Still do not block the rake louvers or the ridge.
- If you want the vented-gap bonus, add intake to the front eave soffit. Balanced venting for 192 sq ft works out to
  roughly 45 sq in of free area low and the same high, which is three or four standard louvered soffit vents — about
  $18 and an hour with a hole saw. This is a small, cheap, specific job that nothing in the current plan covers.

Worth a 30-second check on site: stand at the back, look up, and confirm whether the louver is in the flat eave soffit
(good, and my read is wrong) or in the sloped rake soffit (short circuit).

## 4. Heat: the target is wrong and the fan is the lever

The arithmetic nobody has run yet:

- Room volume is 16 × 12 × 7.25 ft plus the roof triangle, so about 1,400–1,600 cu ft.
- A small vent fan at 100 CFM gives about 4 air changes an hour. That is nothing in a hot box.
- A 12 in gable fan at about 800 CFM is a full air change every two minutes. That is the floor worth wiring.
- A 20 in box fan at 1,500–2,000 CFM is a change every 45–60 seconds. That is what a ventilated shop feels like.
- Intake free area needed, in sq ft, is roughly CFM ÷ 500. At 1,500 CFM that is 3 sq ft. Two 18 × 22 jalousies pass
  roughly 3–4 sq ft wide open, so they are right at the limit — anything bigger and you need a door leaf open.
  If the intake is choked, the fan takes its make-up air from the ridge and the sill gap instead, which means pulling
  hot attic air and sill dust down onto you. That is worse than no fan.

**On the target.** An unvented, unshaded shed runs 15–25 °F above ambient, which is consistent with 105 °F inside on a
low-90s day. Foil plus real ventilation should get you to roughly ambient plus 3–5 °F. On a 92 °F afternoon that is
mid-90s inside, not high 80s. The honest version of the goal is "a few degrees off the shade temperature outside, with a
personal fan on you" — and then the schedule does the rest. At 7am in July the ambient is high 70s. **The clock is the
cheapest cooling in this project and it is free.**

**On what foil actually does.** Its main effect is not air temperature, it is radiant load. A 145 °F deck seven feet over
your head radiates at you like a low broiler; foil sitting at 105 °F does not. The air may only drop 5–10 °F while the
shed feels dramatically better. So buy a $20 infrared thermometer and judge the job by deck-versus-foil surface
temperature, not by the air reading — otherwise a successful install will look like a failure on a thermometer.

**On the paint.** The dominant gain is the dark shingle roof, not the walls, and you are not re-roofing. Lighter siding is
worth maybe 1–3 °F. Repaint for water and UV protection and to seal that raw bottom edge, and do not count on it for the
105 °F problem.

## 5. The foil plane and the fan location are one decision

See [`diagrams/alt-barrier-plane.png`](diagrams/alt-barrier-plane.png).

Your locked plan — foil following the underside of the rafters — is still the better default, and it is what I would do.
It keeps full headroom, keeps the collar ties usable for storage, keeps the room connected to the ridge so warm air can
still stack out, and leaves the fan location free.

But there is a real alternative worth knowing about, because a flat foil plane stapled across the bottom of the collar
ties is dramatically easier to install: one flat plane, long straight runs, no cutting around every tie, about 192 sq ft
instead of 210, and it drops the volume the fan has to move by roughly 12 percent. The costs are that the collar ties
stop being storage, you lose the open-rafter feel, and — the part that is easy to get wrong — **the exhaust fan then has
to be below the foil.** A fan up in the gable triangle above a flat foil plane ventilates an attic you are not standing
in. With the sloped plan, any high spot works.

Either way: buy one 4 ft × 62 ft roll, about 250 sq ft. The sloped roof surface is only about 210 sq ft (16 ft × ~6.3 ft
of slope per side, plus the gable triangles). The 500 sq ft roll is twice what this shed needs.

## 6. Sequence: what I would reorder, and why

The current phases are 0 nest, 1 clear and measure, 2 envelope, 3 heat, 4 power. I would reorder to this, for two
concrete reasons rather than preference — drying takes weeks, and a wet probe gives a false reading:

| | Phase | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Nest | Unchanged. Nothing at the eave happens first. |
| 1 | **Water at the ground** (gravel strip, downspout, grade, pull the leaning panel, shovel the interior berm) | One afternoon, ~$50, and it is the only task with weeks of latency. Start the clock. |
| 2 | Junk out, clear the collar ties, clean | Unchanged, but now the walls are drying while you do it. |
| 3 | **Foil, front eave intake vents, fan** | A few hours and one roll. Every later session is survivable. |
| 4 | Probe, then sister or replace, then screen the gap, doors, paint | The probe is now meaningful, and you only buy the lumber you actually need. |
| 5 | Solar | Unchanged. |

## 7. Doors: the leaf gap and the missing threshold, not just the bottoms

The leaves in [`photos/exterior.jpg`](photos/exterior.jpg) are home-built board-and-batten with a diagonal brace, not
manufactured doors. Two things the current plan does not cover:

- **There is a visible daylight gap between the two leaves**, and no threshold where they meet the slab. A sweep on the
  bottom of each leaf does not address either. You want an astragal on the meeting stiles and a threshold or a stop at
  the slab, then the sweeps. Otherwise that opening stays the biggest hole in the building after you have spent a
  weekend screening a hairline gap at the block line.
- **Check which way the diagonal brace runs, and whether the door binds.** On a braced batten door the diagonal must run
  from the bottom hinge corner up to the top latch corner, or the leaf sags and the bottom drags. A dragging bottom in a
  splash zone rots on schedule. If the brace is backwards, flipping it is cheaper than the repair you would keep
  redoing. While you are there, put a level or a string across the top plate above the 48 in opening; more than about
  1/4 in of dip means the header is undersized and the door will keep binding no matter what you do to the bottoms.

## 8. Structure: what I checked, and what to stop worrying about

Reading this as a framing question rather than a rot question:

- **The roof framing is fine and needs no money.** 2×4 rafters spanning about 6 ft horizontally each side of the ridge,
  with collar ties, is well within capacity for SC roof loads. Do not let anyone talk you into sistering rafters.
- **The walls are heavily over-built for the load.** 74 in 2×4 studs with a double top plate carrying a 12 ft roof span
  is not a concern.
- **A 48 in opening in that eave wall is not a concern either.** Tributary load is roughly 24 sq ft of roof, call it
  600–750 lb, which a doubled 2×6 header carries easily. Only act if you can measure sag.
- **The one real structural item is the hold-down, and the notes treat it as a pest problem.** In
  [`photos/interior-sill.jpg`](photos/interior-sill.jpg) there is an anchor bolt with a plate washer sitting in the
  stained zone. If that washer sinks into soft wood when you press on it, the wall has no uplift or racking restraint at
  that point — which matters far more in a summer storm than a mouse hole does. Check every bolt, not just the wood
  between them. If a bolt has lost its bearing, that plate section gets replaced or a new anchor goes into the block.
- **When you replace plate, use PT and put a capillary break under it** (sill gasket or even 30# felt). If the existing
  2×6 turns out to be untreated wood sitting directly on masonry, that is the root cause, not bad luck.
- **Get the lumber and corrugated panels off the collar ties** — already on your list, and worth doing early. Collar ties
  are tension members; a stack of lumber at mid-span loads them in bending, and it is over your head.

## 9. Power: skip the inverter, skip the roof

- **The best first solar load is the fan, and it is naturally load-matched** — the fan needs the most power at exactly
  the hour the panel makes the most. A 100–200 W panel, a cheap charge controller, one small 12 V LiFePO4, a 12 V DC fan
  and DC LED strips is the whole system. No inverter means no conversion loss, no phantom draw, and nothing to fail.
- **Do not mount panels on the shingle roof.** A gable-end bracket or a simple ground A-frame gives you zero roof
  penetrations on a roof you are not ready to replace, and lets you aim at the sun rather than accept whatever direction
  the ridge happens to run.
- **You may not need solar at all.** A garage-charged power station covers a cleanup session, and the locked "charge in
  the garage" decision is right. Do not store a battery station in a 105 °F shed over the summer either — heat is what
  kills those packs.

## 10. Fitting it out: rails, not sheet goods

The instinct in a shed like this is to line the walls with OSB or plywood so you can hang things anywhere. Do not, at
least not at the bottom. The wall base has been wet; the open interior is its only drying path, and sheet goods over the
studs would seal that off and hide the next round of damage.

Instead, screw two flat 2×4 rails across the studs at roughly hip and shoulder height with 3 in screws into every stud.
That gives you a continuous mounting surface for bike hooks, French cleats, and brackets, for something like $15 instead
of $120, it is stronger than sheet goods, and the wall still dries. The horizontal blocking already visible in
[`photos/interior.jpg`](photos/interior.jpg) can serve as one of them.

Two more cheap wins from the interior photos: the shed already has usable wall cabinets and shelving on the right-hand
wall, so buy less storage than you think. And a coat of white or light paint on the interior OSB gable ends makes a huge
difference to how much light you need out of a battery lamp.

## 11. The nest: dust beats a wet spray for a nest in a void

The plan says dusk-or-dawn nest spray. For a nest in an open void — behind a lifted gutter guard, which is what the
photos suggest — an insecticidal dust is the better tool, because the workers walk it deep into the void and treat the
part you cannot reach. A jetting aerosol mostly knocks down foragers at the entrance.

Three practical points that differ from the note as written:

- Treat at **full dark**, not dusk. Yellow jackets are still flying at dusk in SC summer.
- **Do not seal the entry immediately.** Blocking a treated nest can drive survivors further into the structure. Dust,
  wait 48 hours of no traffic, then open it up, scrape, and reseat the guards.
- **Do not do this from a ladder.** Treat from the ground with reach, and only go up after 48 quiet hours.

And note the cause: a lifted gutter guard is what created that protected void in the first place. Which is another
argument for the gravel strip over more gutter.

## Measurements, and how to take them without a ladder

| What | How | Why it is blocking |
|---|---|---|
| Roof pitch | Hold a phone level app flat against the underside of one rafter and read the angle. Pitch = 12 × tan(angle). Or measure the gable wall from the top plate to the underside of the ridge — that rise over a 6 ft run *is* the pitch. | You do not need the nest gone or a ladder. Sets the foil quantity and any solar layout. |
| Wall-to-block gap | Tape at four or five spots per side, plus both door jambs | Decides whether the cloth is a project or a non-issue. Do not buy the roll first. |
| Is the blue line live | $15 non-contact voltage tester, then trace both ends | Do before stapling foil |
| Plate at the anchor bolts | Thumb pressure and a screwdriver at each bolt, not just between them | The actual structural question |
| Header sag over the door | 4 ft level or a string across the top plate | Explains door binding, if any |
| Vent location, back gable | Look up from the ground: sloped rake soffit or flat eave soffit? | Decides whether the front eave needs intake vents |
| Probe the stud bottoms | Screwdriver, **after several dry weeks** | Wet wood reads soft. Probing now over-diagnoses. |
